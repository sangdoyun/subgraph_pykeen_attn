TEMPORAL PATTERN DETECTION

(Daily / weekly / periodic routines)

Goal (Very Precise)

Detect time-based routines such as:

“User watches Netflix on Friday nights”

“User checks news every weekday morning”

“User orders food late at night”


A temporal pattern means:

> The same action occurs repeatedly at similar times across multiple cycles.




---

Step 0: Raw Data (What the Phone Actually Sees)

The phone observes event logs, e.g.:

(timestamp, event_type, event_id)

Examples:

(2026-01-05 19:12, APP_OPEN, NETFLIX)
(2026-01-06 19:25, APP_OPEN, NETFLIX)
(2026-01-09 19:40, APP_OPEN, NETFLIX)

At this point:

No sessions

No patterns

Just timestamps



---

Step 1: Select Candidate Actions (Noise Reduction)

We do not run temporal detection on everything.

Step 1.1 – Action Filtering

We select actions that:

Occur more than N times in the window (e.g., ≥ 5 times in 30 days)

Are meaningful (apps, calls, payments, media start)


This prevents:

One-off actions

Rare system events


Output of Step 1

A list like:

Candidate actions = {NETFLIX, FOOD_APP, NEWS_APP}


---

Step 2: Time Normalization (Critical Step)

Raw timestamps are hard to compare.
We normalize time into repeatable cycles.

Step 2.1 – Choose Time Resolution

Pick a fixed bin size, e.g.:

30 minutes (good tradeoff)

48 bins per day


This is configurable but fixed.


---

Step 2.2 – Build a Binary Time Series

For each action separately, we create a binary vector:

Example: NETFLIX over 30 days

Day 1:
  18:00–18:30 → 0
  18:30–19:00 → 1
  19:00–19:30 → 1
  …
Day 2:
  18:00–18:30 → 0
  18:30–19:00 → 1
  …

Flattened into:

T_NETFLIX[t] ∈ {0,1}

This is the core representation.


---

Step 3: Autocorrelation (Detect Repetition)

Now we ask a simple but powerful question:

> “Does this timeline look similar to itself after shifting by one day? One week?”




---

Step 3.1 – Define Candidate Periods

We only test human-meaningful periods:

24 hours (daily)

48 hours

7 days (weekly)


This keeps computation cheap and focused.


---

Step 3.2 – Compute Overlap Score (Autocorrelation)

For a given lag τ (e.g., 24h):

Overlap(τ) = Σ T[t] × T[t − τ]

Interpretation:

Counts how often the action occurs at the same time of day on different days


We normalize by number of active days.


---

Example

If Netflix appears around 19:00 on many days:

Overlap at 24h → high

Overlap at random shifts → low


This is not frequency, it’s temporal alignment.


---

Step 4: Phase Localization (Where in the Day?)

Autocorrelation tells us a routine exists, but not when.


---

Step 4.1 – Extract Active Time Bins

For the detected period (say weekly):

Collect all time bins where T[t] = 1



---

Step 4.2 – Build Phase Histogram

Count occurrences by time-of-day:

18:00–18:30 → 2
18:30–19:00 → 5
19:00–19:30 → 6


---

Step 4.3 – Find Stable Time Window

We find:

contiguous bins

with high counts

low variance across weeks


This gives:

Typical time window = 18:30–21:00


---

Step 5: Stability Across Cycles (Avoid False Positives)

A single good week is not enough.


---

Step 5.1 – Split Data into Cycles

For a weekly pattern:

Week 1

Week 2

Week 3

Week 4



---

Step 5.2 – Repeat Detection Per Cycle

We check:

Does the same time window appear in most cycles?


If:

Appears in ≥ K cycles (e.g., 3 out of 4) → pattern is stable



---

Step 6: Confidence Scoring

We compute a confidence score based on:

Overlap strength

Phase consistency

Number of cycles observed


Example:

confidence = 0.82


---

Final Output (What the System Stores)

TemporalPattern {
  action: NETFLIX,
  period: weekly,
  time_window: Friday 18:30–21:00,
  confidence: high
}


---

Why This Is NOT Trivial

✔ Not simple frequency
✔ Requires time alignment across cycles
✔ Robust to missing days
✔ No rules like “Friday = weekend”
✔ Fully data-driven


---

Why This Works on a Smartphone

Binary vectors (cheap)

Only a few lags tested

Incremental updates possible

No FFT or deep models

Memory: O(days × bins)



---

Mental Model (Very Important)

Temporal detection is:

> “Does the same action repeatedly align in time across repeated cycles?”



Not:

“Does it happen often?”

“Does it happen on weekends?”
